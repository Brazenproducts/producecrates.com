FDA Food-Grade Compliance for Reusable Produce Crates

Producecrates Editorial • October 2026

Why Food-Grade Compliance Matters for Produce Handling

For produce operations moving fresh fruit and vegetables from field to packhouse to retail, the container is a food-contact surface. Every crate that touches unwrapped produce falls under the same regulatory expectations as any other food-contact material. Procurement teams that overlook this exposure risk audit findings, rejected loads, and liability when a buyer's food-safety team reviews the supply chain. Reusable plastic crates can meet these standards reliably, but only when the resin, additives, and manufacturing are documented from the start.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration regulates food-contact substances under 21 CFR, and polypropylene used for direct food contact must comply with 21 CFR 177.1520. Crates made from compliant virgin or food-grade resin carry documentation that buyers can request during qualification. This is the first question a serious procurement specialist should ask a supplier.

What to Verify Before You Buy

Qualifying a crate for food-grade service is a paperwork exercise as much as a physical one. Ask every prospective supplier for the following before issuing a purchase order:

  • Confirmation that the resin meets 21 CFR 177.1520 for olefin polymers in food contact
  • A declaration that no non-compliant regrind or recycled post-consumer material touches food surfaces
  • Colorant and additive statements confirming food-grade pigments
  • Migration testing data where the buyer's program requires it
  • Lot traceability so a specific crate run can be tied back to a resin certificate

Growers and co-packers that supply national retailers often inherit the retailer's supplier standards, which can be stricter than the baseline FDA rules. Building compliance documentation into the sourcing process up front avoids scrambling during a customer audit later.

Compliance Is an Ongoing Discipline

Buying compliant crates is step one. Maintaining compliance means keeping the crates clean, inspecting for damage that can harbor contaminants, and retiring units that crack or delaminate. A documented wash-and-inspect protocol closes the loop between the material certificate and day-to-day food safety. Retailers increasingly ask to see that protocol during supplier reviews, so treat it as part of the same compliance package as the resin certificate.

Building a Defensible Program

The strongest produce-crate programs tie three elements together: a food-grade resin certificate on file, a traceable manufacturing source, and a sanitation record that proves the crates stay clean in service. Procurement teams that assemble all three can answer any auditor's question without delay. Working with a direct manufacturer rather than a reseller simplifies this because the documentation originates with the party that controls the resin and the molding process, eliminating the gaps that appear when crates pass through several intermediaries.

For growers weighing a switch from single-use waxed or corrugated containers to reusable crates, food-grade compliance is not a reason to hesitate. Properly sourced PP crates meet the same FDA standards while delivering the durability and reuse economics that single-use packaging cannot. The key is to demand documentation at the quoting stage and verify it before the first load ships.
